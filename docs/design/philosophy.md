# Woven Stone

*A design philosophy for the Museum of Cappadocian Greeks*

**Woven Stone** treats every surface as both carved rock and woven thread. Form is built the way a kilim is built: one knot at a time, on a strict grid, with patience that shows. Space is cut the way a chapel is cut into tuff, by taking material away until only the essential chamber remains. The result should feel quiet, warm and very old, yet exact. Each composition is meticulously crafted, the product of deep expertise and painstaking attention, as if it took a lifetime at the loom.

**Space and form.** Large fields of soft stone-coloured ground, interrupted by precise, dense passages of pattern. Shapes come from three ancestral gestures: the **spiral** (continuity and return), the **lozenge** (the woven eye of the rug) and the **arch** (the rock-cut doorway). Nothing is free-floating decoration: every form sits on an underlying grid and every grid line is honoured. Executed with master-level precision, the pattern should reward close looking: count the steps and they always add up.

**Colour and material.** The palette is taken from objects, not trends. Tuff and pumice provide the ground. Madder red and indigo come from the dyes, ochre gold from the thread on black wool, and a cool silver grey from the church treasures. Colour is used sparingly and deliberately: mostly ground, a disciplined amount of ink, and a single accent of red. Each pairing should look painstakingly calibrated, never accidental.

**Scale and rhythm.** Rhythm comes from repetition, which is the rhythm of weaving and of liturgy. Small units repeat into large fields, and a single mark becomes monumental through accumulation. Contrast between one vast quiet zone and one dense woven band gives the work its pulse. The craftsmanship lies in the counting: spacing is never approximate.

**Hierarchy and text.** Images and pattern speak first. Typography is whispered: a high-contrast serif for the few names that matter, and a thin sans or mono for clinical labels, plate numbers and coordinates, as in a catalogue raisonné. Text is never explanation, only annotation. Every word is placed with the care of a conservator labelling an object, the work of someone at the very top of their field.
